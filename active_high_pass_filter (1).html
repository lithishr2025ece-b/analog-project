1. Introduction and Learning Objectives

An active high-pass filter is an electronic circuit that reduces signals below a selected frequency and passes signals above that frequency. It uses passive components (resistors and capacitors) together with an active device such as an operational amplifier.

Learning objectives

  • Explain why a filter removes DC and low frequencies.
  • Calculate the cutoff frequency.
  • Understand gain and phase response.
  • Design and test a first-order inverting active high-pass filter.
  • Interpret a Multisim frequency-response plot.

Prerequisites

  • Ohm’s law and impedance
  • Capacitor behaviour in DC and AC
  • Ideal op-amp assumptions
  • Decibels and logarithmic frequency axes

Key terms

Cutoff frequency: frequency at which magnitude is 0.707 of the passband value (−3 dB).

Passband: frequencies intended to pass.

Stopband: frequencies attenuated by the filter.

Where is it used?

Audio input conditioning, sensor signal conditioning, removal of slow baseline changes, vibration measurement, and instrumentation front ends. A practical design must also consider noise, op-amp bandwidth, input bias current, supply limits, and component tolerances.

3. Working Principle

  1. At DC (0 Hz): a capacitor behaves approximately as an open circuit after steady state. The input path is blocked, so the output due to the input DC component is strongly reduced.
  2. At low frequencies: the capacitor’s reactance is high, so only a small signal reaches the inverting input through Rin. Output magnitude is low.
  3. At the cutoff frequency: the magnitude is about 0.707 of the passband magnitude, which is −3.01 dB relative to the passband.
  4. At high frequencies: capacitor reactance becomes small. The circuit approaches the inverting amplifier gain, approximately −Rf/Rin.
Capacitive reactance: Xc = 1 / (2π f C) Ω

For an ideal op-amp with negative feedback, the inverting input is approximately at virtual ground and input current flows through the feedback resistor. The input network determines the frequency dependence, while the feedback resistor sets the passband gain.

4. Equations and Design Calculations

Cutoff frequency

fc = 1 / (2π Rin C1)

Where fc is in hertz (Hz), Rin is in ohms (Ω), and C1 is in farads (F).

Rin = 10 kΩ = 10,000 Ω; C1 = 100 nF = 100 × 10⁻⁹ F
fc = 1 / (2π × 10,000 × 100 × 10⁻⁹) ≈ 159.15 Hz

Transfer function and gain

H(s) = Vout/Vin = −(Rf/Rin) · [s Rin C1 / (1 + s Rin C1)]
|H(jω)| = (Rf/Rin) · [f / √(f² + fc²)]

The minus sign indicates inversion. In the passband, where f is much greater than fc, the gain magnitude approaches Rf/Rin.

Passband gain = −Rf/Rin = −10 kΩ / 10 kΩ = −1 V/V
Gain in dB = 20 log₁₀(|Vout/Vin|)

Phase

For this inverting first-order high-pass topology, phase transitions from approximately +90° at very low frequency to approximately −180° (equivalent to +180°) at high frequency, with sign convention depending on how phase is displayed by the simulator. At cutoff, the output is 45° away from its high-frequency inverting phase.

Ideal assumptions

  • Op-amp has sufficiently high open-loop gain and bandwidth.
  • Output does not saturate; input stays within the allowed range.
  • Resistors and capacitor are ideal; parasitic effects are neglected.

5. Solved Numerical Examples

Example 1 — Find cutoff frequency

Given: Rin = 10 kΩ, C = 100 nF. Find: fc.

fc = 1/(2πRC) = 1/[2π × 10,000 × 100 × 10⁻⁹] = 159.15 Hz

Answer: approximately 159 Hz. Signals much lower than this are attenuated; signals well above it approach the passband gain.

Example 2 — Find the passband gain

Given: Rin = 10 kΩ and Rf = 22 kΩ.

Av ≈ −Rf/Rin = −22/10 = −2.2 V/V
Gain(dB) = 20 log₁₀(2.2) ≈ 6.85 dB

Answer: −2.2 V/V, or 6.85 dB in magnitude. The negative sign indicates inversion.

Example 3 — GATE-style: magnitude at cutoff

Given: passband gain magnitude = 1 and f = fc.

|H| = 1 × fc/√(fc² + fc²) = 1/√2 ≈ 0.707
20 log₁₀(0.707) ≈ −3.01 dB

Answer: 0.707 V/V, which is −3 dB relative to the passband.

8. Core Electronics Interview Questions

1. What is an active high-pass filter?

A filter that attenuates low frequencies and passes higher frequencies using an active component such as an op-amp along with resistors and capacitors.

2. What does the cutoff frequency mean?

It is the frequency where the output magnitude is 1/√2 (about 0.707) of the passband magnitude, equal to −3 dB.

3. Why does a capacitor block DC?

After charging in steady-state DC, the ideal capacitor current becomes zero. Its impedance magnitude is very high as frequency approaches zero.

4. How can you increase the cutoff frequency?

Reduce Rin, reduce C, or reduce both, because fc = 1/(2πRinC).

5. What sets the passband gain?

For the inverting topology, the magnitude is approximately Rf/Rin when frequency is well above cutoff.

6. What is the role of negative feedback?

It stabilizes closed-loop gain and helps the op-amp operate linearly, provided the output is not saturated and the device has adequate bandwidth.

7. What is the difference between a passive and active filter?

A passive filter uses passive elements only. An active filter uses an active device and can provide gain or buffering, but requires power and has bandwidth limits.

8. Why might Multisim show an unexpected peak?

Check the actual wiring, whether Rin is present, instrument input/output terminal connections, sweep limits, op-amp model, supply pins, and the selected magnitude/phase display.

9. What happens if the op-amp output saturates?

The output clips at a supply-related limit, so the linear filter equations no longer predict the waveform correctly.

10. How would you test the filter in a lab?

Apply a small sine wave, sweep frequency logarithmically, measure input and output amplitude, calculate gain in dB, and identify the −3 dB point relative to the passband.

11. Why are component tolerances important?

Cutoff depends on R and C. Their tolerances shift the actual cutoff from the nominal value.

12. What practical op-amp limits matter?

Gain-bandwidth product, slew rate, input common-mode range, output swing, noise, bias currents, and supply voltage.

Advantages

  • Can attenuate DC and slow signal changes.
  • Op-amp can provide buffering or gain.
  • Cutoff frequency can be adjusted by changing R or C.

Limitations

  • Needs a power supply.
  • Real op-amp bandwidth, noise and output swing limit performance.
  • Component tolerances alter the cutoff.
  • A first-order filter has a gradual roll-off of about 20 dB/decade below cutoff.

Common mistakes

  • Using kΩ and nF without converting units.
  • Calling the −3 dB point the point where gain is zero.
  • Forgetting the inverting sign when discussing phase.
  • Reading a plot peak as cutoff without checking the circuit and sweep.
  • Assuming the provided screenshot proves the theoretical response, even when the wiring differs.
